Part 01

What a Mutual Fund Actually Is — the pooling engine

The textbook definition, the unit, the ₹10 offer price, and the one idea the whole chapter hangs on.

A small saver in Bajali with ₹5,000 cannot buy a diversified portfolio of thirty companies, cannot read a balance sheet, and cannot watch the market all day. A mutual fund exists to solve exactly that problem — it collects thousands of such ₹5,000s and hands the combined pile to somebody who can.

The definition you write in the exam

Your textbook opens the chapter with this, and it is the sentence to reproduce:

Textbook definition — ASSEB Finance, Ch. 16Mutual fund is a collective investment created through mutual contributions made by large numbers of small savers who have no expertise knowledge to undertake investment in equities successfully.

And the supporting line, which is worth adding as a second sentence in any 2-mark or 5-mark answer: mutual fund is basically an investment vehicle through which the fund managers pool fund to invest in shares and debentures. Financial institutions, private companies and public companies all act as fund managers.

Read it left to right: many small contributions → one corpus → one professional manager → many securities → returns back to the same small savers. The whole chapter is footnotes to this picture.

The unit — the single most examinable noun in the chapter

The fund created by the contributions of investors is divided into equal portions called a unit. A unit is your proportionate ownership slice of the scheme. The price of each unit is governed by the value of the underlying investments — and the initial offer price of each unit is basically ₹10.

The ₹10 Trap

Students write "the price of a unit is ₹10." Wrong tense. ₹10 is the initial offer price only — the price at launch. After launch the unit price is governed by NAV, which moves with the value of the underlying securities. Write "initial offer price" or lose the mark.

Features of a mutual fund (the 2-mark construction)

The chapter has no heading called "features" — but AHSEC asked for two of them in 2023. Build them out of the meaning paragraph:

  1. Pooled / collective investment — money from a large number of small savers is combined into one corpus.
  2. Divided into units — the corpus is split into equal portions called units; initial offer price basically ₹10.
  3. NAV-based pricing — unit value is governed by the value of the underlying investments, measured by Net Asset Value.
  4. Professional management — the fund is managed by experienced and skilled professionals, not by the investor.
  5. Diversification — investment is spread across a number of companies and instruments.
  6. Flexibility — SIP, SWP and STP facilities allow flexible entry, exit and switching.
  7. Regulated — all mutual funds are registered with SEBI.
Exam Lens — PYQ evidence

ASSEB 2023, Q3 (2 marks): "Write two features of Mutual Fund." Two features, one line each. Convenience and flexibility of investment were the sanctioned answers that year, but any two from the list above are defensible.

For 1-mark slots the chapter's own question list asks: full form of SIP, full form of NAV, the first mutual fund institution of India, and the year UTI was established. Four gimmes.

Part 02

NAV — Net Asset Value

The one calculation in this chapter. Learn the formula, then use the live calculator below.

NAV is the price tag on a unit. It is also the only place in Chapter 16 where an examiner can ask you to compute something — which makes it disproportionately valuable, because most candidates only memorise words.

The formula, stated the textbook's way

Your textbook words it as: total value of a scheme minus liabilities and then divided by outstanding number of units calculates the NAV. In symbols:

Net Asset Value — ASSEB Finance Ch. 16 Total value of the scheme − Liabilities NAV = ───────────────────────────────────────────── Number of outstanding units
Order of operations: subtract first, divide second. Getting this backwards is the standard arithmetic slip.

Worked Example — the classic setup

Problem: A scheme's investments are valued at ₹52,00,000. Its liabilities are ₹2,00,000. It has 5,00,000 units outstanding. Find the NAV per unit.

Step 1 — Net assets: ₹52,00,000 − ₹2,00,000 = ₹50,00,000.

Step 2 — Divide by units: ₹50,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000 units.

Step 3 — Compute: 50,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000 = 10.

Answer: NAV = ₹10 per unit — the scheme is exactly at its initial offer price.

Worked Example — reading the return

Problem: Kapil buys 1,000 units at the initial offer price of ₹10. Two years later the NAV is ₹14. What is his position?

Step 1 — Cost: 1,000 × ₹10 = ₹10,000.

Step 2 — Present value: 1,000 × ₹14 = ₹14,000.

Step 3 — Gain: ₹14,000 − ₹10,000 = ₹4,000, i.e. 4,000 ÷ 10,000 = 40% appreciation.

Answer: the number of units never changed — only the NAV did. That is the entire logic of capital appreciation in a mutual fund.

Why NAV, and not "market price"

An open-ended unit is not traded on an exchange, so there is no bid and ask to set its price. The only honest price is what the underlying portfolio is worth per unit right now — which is precisely NAV. This is also why open-ended funds transact directly with the AMC at NAV, while closed-ended units, being listed, can trade on the exchange at a price that differs from NAV.

The "divide by units first" Trap

Under time pressure students compute Assets ÷ Units and then subtract liabilities. That is wrong — liabilities are a claim on the pool, not on each unit. Always net the liabilities off the scheme's total value before dividing.

Exam Lens

The chapter's own 2-mark question is "Explain how NAV is calculated?" — one formula line plus one sentence of explanation is a full answer. Write the formula as a proper fraction; examiners reward the layout.

Part 03

The Five Parties — who does what

Sponsor, Trustee, Custodian, AMC, Beneficial Owner. A guaranteed 2-marker, and the backbone of any long answer's introduction.

A mutual fund is not one organisation. It is a trust structure with deliberately separated roles, so that the person who promotes the fund is not the person who holds its securities, and neither of them is the person who decides what to buy.

The list, in the textbook's order

PartyRole
SponsorThe promoter — the institution or company that establishes the mutual fund and gets it registered.
TrusteeHolds the fund in trust for the investors and supervises that the scheme is run in the investors' interest.
CustodianHolds the securities of the scheme in safe custody and handles their delivery and settlement.
Asset Management Company (AMC)The fund manager — takes the actual investment decisions and manages the portfolio for a fee.
Beneficial Owner (Investors)The unit-holders. They own the beneficial interest in the fund; the returns belong to them.
Draw this if a long answer asks for the structure. Five boxes, one dotted line from the regulator, and you have earned the presentation marks.
Why the Custodian is separated from the AMC

If the same entity both decided what to buy and physically held what was bought, there would be nothing stopping it from quietly moving securities out. Splitting decision-making (AMC) from custody (Custodian) under a supervising Trustee is the structural safeguard that makes the SEBI-registered fund a "protected investment" — which is exactly benefit no. 7 in Part 8.

The Sponsor–Trustee Trap

Students collapse Sponsor and Trustee into one. They are distinct: the Sponsor establishes the fund; the Trustee holds it in trust and supervises it on behalf of unit-holders. If a 2-mark question says "name the parties," name all five — partial lists get partial marks.

Exam Lens

"What are the various parties involved in Mutual fund?" is a listed 2-mark question in your textbook exercise. Five names, no explanation needed at 2 marks. At 3 or 5 marks, add the one-line role for each — the table above is exactly that answer.

Recall check — tap to reveal
  1. AMC stands for Asset Management Company.
  2. The party that holds the scheme's securities in safe custody is the Custodian.
  3. The investors are described in the textbook as the Beneficial Owners.
Part 04

Classification I — on the basis of Operation

Closed-ended, Open-ended, Interval. Three schemes, one question of whether the door stays open.

The textbook classifies mutual funds twice, on two independent bases. The first — execution or operation — asks a single question: can you get in and out whenever you like? Everything else follows from the answer.

(i) Closed-ended funds

Under this scheme the corpus of the fund is fixed and the fund's life is pre-fixed. The units are redeemable or returnable to investors only after expiry of the term. Entry and exit of investors from the fund is not allowed once the scheme is closed. However, an existing investor who wishes to sell his holdings before the date of maturity may do so in the stock exchange where the units are listed.

(ii) Open-ended funds

Under this scheme the fund size and the period of investment are not pre-fixed. A person willing to invest can invest at any time and can also redeem his investment as and when required. The purchase and sale of units in an open-ended fund is not restricted — investors buy from, or sell holdings directly to, the AMC issuing the units.

(iii) Interval funds

Interval funds combine the features of open-ended and closed-ended schemes. These funds are open for purchase or redemption during specific intervals at Net Asset Value and closed the rest of the time. The units under this scheme are traded in the stock exchange.

Green = you may transact. Muted = you may not. Interval funds are literally open-ended behaviour switched on and off on a schedule.
Why closed-ended units must be listed

If the AMC will not buy your units back before maturity, you need somebody else to buy them — and that somebody is found on a stock exchange. Listing is not a decoration; it is the only exit route a closed-ended investor has. Open-ended funds do not need listing precisely because the AMC itself is always the buyer.

The "closed means frozen" Trap

Students write that a closed-ended investor "cannot sell at all until maturity." Incomplete — and it costs half the mark. The correct line is: he cannot redeem from the fund, but he may sell the units on the stock exchange where they are listed.

Exam Lens

"Explain various types of Mutual fund on the basis of Operation or Execution" is a listed 5-mark question in your textbook. Three types, so budget roughly: one line of definition + two lines of explanation each, plus a one-line opening. Do not pad — 5-mark answers reward density.

Part 05

Open-ended vs Closed-ended — the table you must not improvise

Six bases, straight from the textbook. This is a distinguish question, so it is answered in a table. Always.

AHSEC loves "distinguish between." The rule you already apply everywhere in this subject applies here with no exceptions: a differences question is answered as a comparison table with a stated basis for each row — never as prose.

2. Size of corpusThe size of the corpus is not fixed.The maximum size of the fund is pre-fixed and cannot be exceeded.

Exam Lens — 5 marks, 5 rows

"Distinguish between Open-ended and Closed-ended mutual fund" is a listed 5-mark question. Five marks means five rows minimum — the textbook gives you six, so you have one spare. Rule the table before you start writing, label the columns, and put the basis of difference in column one. A prose answer to a distinguish question loses marks even when the content is right.

The Listing Inversion Trap

Under pressure, half the class swaps rows 3 and 4. Anchor it with the logic from Part 4: closed-ended is the one that is listed, because listing is its only exit. Open-ended is not listed because it needs no exchange — the AMC buys back directly.

Part 06

Classification II — on the basis of Yield & Investment Pattern

Nine fund types. This is your 8-mark question, and it is the densest content in the chapter.

The second classification asks a different question entirely — not when can I exit, but what does the fund actually buy, and what does it do with the income. Nine answers, each with a distinct investor profile attached.

The nine types

#TypeWhat it does
iGrowth-oriented fundIncome generated by the invested fund is not distributed among unit-holders; it is allocated proportionately towards the initial investment, so the size of the unit-holder's investment goes on increasing.
iiIncome-oriented fundAims at distribution of the income generated by the investments among unit-holders, as dividend. Popular with small investors who prefer regular income and avoid high risk — for example, retired persons.
iiiBalanced mutual fundInvested both in equities and in interest-bearing instruments such as debentures and preference shares — so investors get the chance of appreciation in equities plus an ascertained return.
ivStock fundInvested in securities of different companies, varying from highly rated companies to newly established ones. Suitable for those who can resist significant risk for higher returns.
vLeverage fundCombines borrowed funds with own funds and invests in speculative stock that promises higher future profit. Interest on the borrowed fund is paid out of profit earned; the balance is distributed among unit-holders.
viTax relief fundAlso known as equity-linked savings schemes; essentially closed-ended. Investment is made in equity shares that entitle investors to deduction or rebate in income tax under the Income Tax Act, 1961. Subject to a minimum lock-in period.
viiBonds fundBonds are more liquid, diversified and conservative, with modest capital gains; some are sold at a discount to face value. Bonds selected are highly secured with steady income and lesser default risk. Popular with investors who want fixed return plus safety.
viiiMoney market mutual fund (MMMF)Set up to invest in money-market instruments — treasury bills, certificates of deposit, commercial papers, bill discounting. Investments in MMMF are subject to guidelines laid down by the Reserve Bank of India.
ixReal estate fundInvested in real-estate ventures. Basically closed-ended. Such funds are of various types depending upon the real-estate transactions involved.
The Summary-vs-Body Trap — read this one twice

The chapter's main body lists nine types (i–ix, ending with real estate funds). The chapter's Summary at the end lists only eight (a–h) — it drops real estate. Both are in your book. For an 8-mark answer, write eight fully explained types and add real estate as the ninth; you cannot be penalised for including something the main text contains.

Positioning is illustrative, not textbook data — it is a memory aid built from each type's stated investor profile. Do not quote coordinates in an answer; use it to recall which investor each fund suits.

Worked Example — why a leverage fund raises unit-holder return

Problem: A leverage fund has own funds of ₹60,00,000 and borrows ₹40,00,000 at 10% p.a., investing the whole ₹1,00,00,000. The portfolio earns 18% in the year. What return do unit-holders get?

Step 1 — Total earnings: ₹1,00,00,000 × 18% = ₹18,00,000.

Step 2 — Interest on borrowed fund: ₹40,00,000 × 10% = ₹4,00,000.

Step 3 — Balance for unit-holders: ₹18,00,000 − ₹4,00,000 = ₹14,00,000.

Step 4 — Return on own funds: ₹14,00,000 ÷ ₹60,00,000 = 23.33%, against 18% without borrowing.

Answer: leverage lifted the unit-holders' return from 18% to 23.33% — and that same leverage would magnify a loss just as sharply, which is why the textbook calls the underlying stock "speculative."

Growth vs Income — one word decides it

Both funds earn income. The difference is a single verb: growth funds retain it (added back to your investment, so your holding grows), income funds distribute it (paid out as dividend). Everything else — who invests, what risk, which age group — follows from that verb.

Exam Lens — the 8-marker

"Explain various types of Mutual fund on the basis of Investment pattern" is the chapter's only listed 8-mark question. Structure it: two-line introduction → eight numbered types with two to three lines each → one-line conclusion. Roughly 300 words. Do not write eight one-word bullets; 8-mark answers are graded on explanation, not on the list.

Part 07

SIP, SWP and STP — the flexibility trio

Three full forms the examiner can ask for in one mark each, plus the arithmetic that makes SIP worth understanding.

Your textbook says mutual funds offer flexibility in investment pattern, and names three facilities that deliver it. These are the cheapest marks in the chapter — one mark each for a full form — and the fastest to forget, because they are only mentioned once.

AbbreviationFull formWhat it lets you do
SIPSystematic Investment PlanInvest a fixed sum at fixed regular intervals instead of one lump sum.
SWPSystematic Withdrawal PlanWithdraw a fixed sum at fixed regular intervals instead of redeeming everything at once.
STPSystematic Transfer PlanTransfer a fixed sum at fixed intervals from one scheme to another within the same fund house.
Worked Example — why SIP averages your cost down

Problem: An investor puts ₹2,000 into a scheme on the first of each month for three months. The NAV on those dates is ₹10, ₹8 and ₹12.50. Compare his average cost per unit with the average NAV.

Step 1 — Units bought each month: 2,000 ÷ 10 = 200 units; 2,000 ÷ 8 = 250 units; 2,000 ÷ 12.50 = 160 units.

Step 2 — Totals: total invested = ₹6,000; total units = 200 + 250 + 160 = 610 units.

Step 3 — Average cost per unit: 6,000 ÷ 610 = ₹9.84 (approx).

Step 4 — Simple average of the three NAVs: (10 + 8 + 12.50) ÷ 3 = 30.50 ÷ 3 = ₹10.17 (approx).

Answer: his cost per unit (₹9.84) is lower than the average NAV (₹10.17), because the fixed rupee amount automatically bought more units when the NAV was low and fewer when it was high.

The bar heights are units, not rupees. The rupee amount is identical every month — only the unit count moves, inversely to NAV.

Part 08

Benefits of Investing in Mutual Funds — the highest-frequency question in the chapter

Eight benefits. Asked in 2019, asked again in 2024. Learn these to the point of reflex.

If you memorise one list from Chapter 16, make it this one. It is the only content in the chapter with a confirmed repeat across the PYQ archive — and the repeat is almost verbatim.

Exam Lens — confirmed PYQ repeat

AHSEC 2019, Q14 (5 marks, alternative): "Mention five advantages of Mutual Funds."
ASSEB 2024, Q10 (5 marks, alternative): "Write the advantages of investing in mutual funds."

Two appearances, five years apart, same 5-mark slot, effectively the same stem. This is the single most probable Mutual Fund question on your paper. Five benefits with one explanatory line each is a full-mark answer; you know eight, so you can pick your strongest five.

01
Liquidity
Investment in mutual fund provides easy liquidity to those who want to dispose of their units. Open-ended funds may be sold back to the AMC; closed-ended funds can be sold in the stock exchange where they are listed.
02
Risk diversification
The fund is invested in diversified portfolios and in a number of companies. This reduces the risk of investment, because stocks seldom decline in value in the same proportion.
03
Return
Investment in mutual fund provides higher return potential to investors, as the fund is invested carefully in selected securities.
04
Professional management
The fund is invested in selected securities by experienced and skilled professionals who have experience and knowledge of portfolio management.
05
Convenience
Investment is very simple — it does not involve much paperwork, and avoids problems such as delayed payments, bad deliveries and unnecessary follow-up with brokers.
06
Less costly
Because of the large scale of investment, brokerage, paperwork, custodial and other charges translate into lower expense for each investor.
07
Protected investment
All mutual funds are registered with SEBI and function within strict regulations designed to protect the interest of investors.
08
Tax benefit
Many mutual funds offer the benefit of tax exemptions to investors under the Income Tax Act.
The Missing Eighth Trap

The chapter's main body lists eight benefits. The Summary at the end lists only seven — it drops Protected investment (the SEBI point). If you revise only from the Summary you will walk into the exam one benefit short. Revise from the main body.

Order them by strength, not by memory

At 5 marks you write five, not eight. Pick the five that are hardest to argue with and easiest to explain in one line: Risk diversification, Professional management, Liquidity, Less costly, Protected investment (SEBI). "Return" is weak on its own — a mutual fund does not guarantee return — so lead with the structural benefits and keep return as a supporting point.

Part 09

Mutual Funds in India — the dated timeline

Names and years. This is where 1-mark questions come from, so the dates have to be exact.

This section of the chapter is almost pure factual recall — a handful of institutions and the years they entered the industry. It is the easiest content in Chapter 16 to score on and the easiest to fumble, because a year that is one digit wrong is a mark gone.

THE IDEA
The need for institutionalisation of mutual funds was felt first by Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru, former Prime Minister of the country.
1963
The idea began to take shape with the enactment of the Unit Trust of India Act, 1963.
The Act comes first. The institution follows a year later — this gap is the trap.
1964
Unit Trust of India (UTI) was established, to operate as a financial institution and an investment trust.
In 1964 the board of trustees formulated an open-ended scheme and launched it at a nominal value of ₹10 each.
1987
Commercial banks were permitted to enter: State Bank of India (1987) and Canara Bank (1987).
1989
Life Insurance Corporation (LIC) entered the mutual fund business.
1990
Indian Bank, Bank of India and Punjab National Bank launched their mutual funds.
1991
General Insurance Corporation (GIC) entered. Public sector units enjoyed a monopoly in the industry till 1991.
Private players were allowed with liberalisation and privatisation, which gained momentum with the financial market reforms from 1991 onwards.
1993
The first private sector mutual fund, pioneering in the field, was Kothari, which launched its mutual fund in 1993.
Later joined by Morgan Stanley, Jardine Fleming, George Soros and Capital International.
The 1963 / 1964 Trap

The Act is 1963. The institution is 1964. If the question says "In which year was UTI established?" the answer is 1964. If it says "the Unit Trust of India Act," the answer is 1963. Read which noun the question is pointing at before you write the year.

Exam Lens — the 1-mark harvest

Two of the chapter's four printed 1-mark questions come from this section: "Write the first Mutual Fund institution of India" (Unit Trust of India) and "In which year the UTI was established?" (1964). A third likely stem is the first private sector mutual fund (Kothari, 1993). Three marks sitting in one page of text.

Why 1991 is the hinge

Notice the shape of the timeline: 1964 to 1987 is UTI alone; 1987 to 1991 is public sector banks and insurers; 1993 onwards is private and foreign. The break is the liberalisation of 1991 — the same reform wave that appears in your Banking chapters. If an 8-mark question ever asks you to trace the growth of mutual funds in India, that three-phase structure is your answer skeleton.

Model answer — "Write a short note on mutual funds in India" (5 marks)

Opening: The need for institutionalisation of mutual funds in India was felt first by Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru.

Point 1 — Origin: The idea took shape in 1963 with the enactment of the Unit Trust of India Act, 1963, and UTI was established in 1964 as a financial institution and investment trust, launching an open-ended scheme at ₹10 per unit.

Point 2 — Public sector expansion: Commercial banks and insurance companies were then permitted to enter — SBI and Canara Bank in 1987, LIC in 1989, Indian Bank, Bank of India and PNB in 1990, and GIC in 1991.

Point 3 — Monopoly: Public sector units enjoyed a monopoly in the industry till 1991.

Point 4 — Private entry: Private players were permitted following liberalisation and the financial market reforms from 1991; Kothari became the first private sector mutual fund in 1993.

Point 5 — Foreign entry: Morgan Stanley, Jardine Fleming, George Soros and Capital International subsequently joined the business.

The kill-list — twelve ways this chapter loses marks

  1. Writing "the price of a unit is ₹10." ₹10 is the initial offer price only. After launch, price follows NAV.
  2. Dividing before subtracting in NAV. Net the liabilities off the scheme's total value first, then divide by units.
  3. Saying a closed-ended investor cannot sell before maturity. He cannot redeem from the fund — he can sell on the stock exchange where the units are listed.
  4. Swapping the listing row. Closed-ended units are listed; open-ended units are not. Anchor it: listing is the closed-ended investor's only exit.
  5. Answering a "distinguish" question in prose. Distinguish always means a ruled table with the basis of difference in column one.
  6. Giving four rows for a 5-mark table. Five marks, five rows. The textbook gives six bases — you have a spare.
  7. Revising the types from the Summary. The Summary lists eight types; the main body lists nine (real estate fund is dropped from the Summary).
  8. Revising the benefits from the Summary. The Summary lists seven; the main body lists eight (protected investment / SEBI is dropped from the Summary).
  9. Confusing growth with income funds. Growth retains the income and adds it to your investment; income distributes it as dividend.
  10. Answering 1964 for the Unit Trust of India Act. The Act is 1963; the institution is 1964. Read which noun the question names.
  11. Attributing MMMF regulation to SEBI. Mutual funds are registered with SEBI, but MMMF investments are subject to guidelines laid down by the RBI. Both appear in this chapter; do not merge them.
  12. Listing eight benefits as bare words for a 5-mark question. Five well-explained beats eight naked. One point plus one line of explanation each.

Presentation marks — free, and most people leave them

  • Rule your comparison table with a scale before writing. Three columns: Basis / Open-ended / Closed-ended.
  • Number your points (i), (ii), (iii) — never dashes or bullets in a board answer.
  • Underline the name of each benefit or fund type; leave the explanation un-underlined. The examiner's eye finds the count instantly.
  • Write the NAV formula as a proper fraction with a horizontal line, not inline with a slash.
  • For the structure of a mutual fund, a small labelled box diagram earns credit at 8 marks and costs ninety seconds.
